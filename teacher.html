Teacher script and notes

The Urban Game: Chicago, 1865 to 1900

Students run one copy each of the same West Side ward. Every copy gets the same arrivals, the same events, and the same fire. Only their choices differ, so differences between wards at the end come from decisions. Open the game | Print this script

Running it

Two ways to playOn my own: the clock never stops except for newspaper pop-ups. Decisions run on a 45 second countdown and default if ignored.
With my teacher: the game stops at the end of each of the seven rounds below and waits. You read the round intro, say "begin," students press Begin. At the end of a round the screen shows discussion questions and locks building, but students can still move the camera and switch map views.
Time
GroupsOne or two students per Chromebook works best. With pairs, try one "Builder" on the trackpad and one "Commissioner" who watches the meters and reads the newspaper aloud.
Before classHave each Chromebook load the game once while online. After that it will open without a connection. Sound can be switched off on the start screen. A game in progress is saved once per game year on that Chromebook, and the start screen offers to continue it.
Preview an eraAdd ?skip=1871 (any year) to the address to jump straight there with default choices. Add ?lowgfx if a machine struggles.
What students controlThey zone land (housing, industry, shops) for free, and builders fill it in as demand allows. They pay for sewers, paving, streetcars, and public buildings. They do not choose what gets built on private land. Unzoned empty land attracts squatter shanties. Crowded lots get rear houses, then tenements, whether students want them or not.
Map views to call outNationalities (compare to the Hull House maps), Crowding, Death risk, Sewers + paving. Street view plus Take photograph lets students document conditions the way Jacob Riis did. Photographs print in their final report.
The endIn December 1900 each student gets a printable report: report card, charts, their decisions, their photographs, and written prompts on the environmental, economic, cultural, and social effects of urbanization, ending with a claim on whether urban growth benefited American society.

The script

Shaded boxes are read-aloud text. It matches what students see in the newspaper pop-ups, so you can read it or let them read it.

Where the game simplifies history

  • The map is a simplified grid of the Near West Side. Street names are real, but there are fewer of them, and the ward is not drawn to the 19th Ward's legal boundaries.
  • The Great Fire started on DeKoven Street, as in the game, but the wind carried it across the river within hours and most of the West Side survived. The game lets it burn whatever a student has built in the wind's path, which is usually more than really burned here.
  • Decisions such as the brick-only fire limits, strike policing, and relief were made by the city council, the mayor, and private charities, not by one ward. The game hands them to students so they feel the trade-offs.
  • Numbers (population, death rates, crowding, money) come from a model tuned to feel right for the period. They are not historical statistics for the 19th Ward. The citywide figures in the final report (298,977 people in 1870 and 1,698,575 in 1900) are from the census.
  • Nationalities are limited to seven groups. The real neighborhood was more mixed, and included a small African American community that the game does not model.
  • Hull House, Johnny Powers, the garbage inspector job, and the 1898 election are real. Students can beat Powers in the game. Hull House never did.
